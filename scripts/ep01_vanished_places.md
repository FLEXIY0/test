Close your eyes for a moment. It's a Saturday morning, and you're maybe twelve years old. Mom just dropped you off at the mall — the one with the marble fountain in the center court, the record store where you'd flip through albums for hours, and that warm smell of fresh pretzels drifting from the food court. You had a couple of dollars in your pocket and the whole afternoon ahead of you. That mall is gone now. Today we're going back to seven places from our childhood that vanished forever. Stay to the end — number three might just bring a tear to your eye.

[IMAGE: woolworth lunch counter interior 1960s soda fountain vintage photo]
First, the five-and-dime lunch counter. Woolworth's, Kresge's, Ben Franklin — every downtown had one. You'd climb onto a swiveling chrome stool and order a cherry Coke mixed right there from syrup and soda water, maybe a grilled cheese and a scoop of ice cream for pocket change. The waitress knew the regulars by name. It wasn't fancy, but it was ours — a place where neighbors nodded hello and a kid could feel grown up spending his own allowance. Those counters closed one by one, and with them went a little of the way small towns used to gather.

[IMAGE: drive-in movie theater at dusk 1970s cars vintage photo]
Then there was the drive-in theater. You'd pile into the back seat in your pajamas, Dad would hook that heavy metal speaker onto the window, and the whole family watched a double feature under the stars. Kids ran between the cars before the show, the snack bar smelled of popcorn and hot dogs, and half the neighborhood was parked in the next row over. Most of us fell asleep before the second movie ended. The screens that dotted the edge of every town have mostly gone dark now, the lots turned into parking or shopping centers.

[IMAGE: full service gas station attendant 1960s vintage photo]
Remember pulling into a full-service gas station? A young man in a crisp uniform and cap would jog out before you'd even stopped rolling. He'd fill your tank, check the oil, wipe down the windshield, and hand you an S&H Green Stamp with a smile — all while you stayed right in your seat. Filling up was a small courtesy between neighbors, not a chore. Today you pump your own, tap a card, and drive off without a word to a soul.

[IMAGE: local soda fountain drugstore 1950s vintage interior]
The corner drugstore soda fountain deserves its own place on this list. It was where teenagers shared a malted with two straws, where you'd spin on the stool waiting for a prescription, and where the pharmacist knew your whole family. A root beer float cost a dime and came in a heavy glass mug. It was the town's living room. When the big chains arrived, those fountains were the first thing to go.

[IMAGE: video rental store interior 1980s shelves vhs vintage]
For those of us a little younger, Friday night meant one thing: the video store. You'd wander the aisles, box in hand, arguing over which movie to rent, hoping the last copy of the new release hadn't already been snatched up. The smell of plastic cases, the little handwritten staff-pick cards, the promise of "be kind, rewind." It was an outing, not a click. Every one of those stores is gone now, and somehow the choosing was half the fun.

[IMAGE: children playing outside neighborhood street 1960s dusk vintage photo]
And number seven isn't a building at all — it's the long summer evening outside. The whole neighborhood's kids in one yard until the streetlights flickered on, that being the only clock that mattered. Games invented on the spot, skinned knees, and a mother's voice calling you home for supper carrying down the whole block. That world of unhurried, wide-open evenings has quietly slipped away too.

[IMAGE: vintage sunset over small american town rooftops warm nostalgic]
These places are gone. But here's the thing — as long as we remember them, that mall, that summer, and those younger versions of ourselves are still alive. So tell me in the comments: which of these do you miss the most, and what did I leave off the list?
